Goal

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, so ΔU = 0 in an isothermal process. Yet we did work W with the piston. Where did that energy go?

Try it

  1. With "Diathermal" and "Slow", compress. W grows but ΔU ≈ 0. What happens to −Q (the energy received by the surroundings)?
  2. "Track one particle": the particle speeds up when it hits the piston and hands energy to the surroundings when it hits a diathermal wall (blue rings). This is what "heat" is, seen microscopically.
  3. Expand. Now the gas does work on the outside (W < 0) and heat flows in from the surroundings (more red rings).
  4. Switch to "Adiabatic" and do the same compression. ΔU = W, and the temperature rises.
Question The work W a person does with the piston is up to them. But the escape of that energy to the surroundings as heat happens regardless of our will. Explain this in connection with "the nature of heat" from the lecture.
Question During isothermal compression, ΔU is not exactly 0 but fluctuates slightly. Why? (What if the number of particles were infinitely large?)
Theoretical values (2D ideal gas)

For an isothermal quasi-static change V₀ → V₁, W = NkT ln(V₀/V₁). With N = 300 and T = 1, halving the volume gives W = 300 × ln 2 ≈ 208 and Q = −W. In the simulation it comes out a few percent larger because of the particle size and the finite piston speed.
