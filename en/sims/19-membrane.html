Goal

In the sedimentation equilibrium of 11, particles split as ctop/cbottom = e−ΔE/kT from the balance between "energy that depends on height" and "the tendency to spread out (configurational entropy)". For ions, the electrical energy zeψ plays the role of gravity (z: valence, e: elementary charge).

The condition for an ion to be in equilibrium across the membrane is that the electrochemical potential μ̃ = μ° + kT ln c + zeψ is the same inside and out. From it follows the Nernst equation ψin − ψout = (kT/ze) ln(cout/cin) (textbook section 4.9).

Try it

  1. Play with Δψ = 0 and "K⁺ only". K⁺ stays at the same concentration inside and out (ln(cin/cout) ≈ 0).
  2. Set Δψ to −1 (inside at a lower potential). The positive K⁺ gathers inside and stops at ln(cin/cout) ≈ +1. The push to spread outward from the concentration difference balances the pull inward from the potential.
  3. "Record present values" at various Δψ. Do the points lie on the line of slope −1 (the Nernst equation for K⁺)?
  4. Switch to "K⁺ and Cl⁻". The negative Cl⁻ splits the opposite way from K⁺ and lies on the line of slope +1. A⁻ cannot cross the membrane, so it stays inside.
  5. Set the initial state to "Like a cell" and play with Δψ = 0. K⁺ moves out (μ̃in > μ̃out). Press "Set to the Nernst potential of K⁺" midway and the net movement stops.
  6. With "None", nothing happens whatever the concentration or potential difference. Equilibrium conditions hold only for the quantities allowed to be exchanged (the same as the partitions in 12).
Question In a nerve cell the K⁺ concentration is about 140 mM inside and 5 mM outside. At body temperature (kT/e ≈ 26.7 mV), what is the Nernst potential of K⁺ in mV? Is the inside positive or negative?
Question Compare the sedimentation formula of 11, ctop/cbottom = e−mgh/kT, with the Nernst equation. What corresponds to mgh? Why is the sign opposite for K⁺ and Cl⁻?
Question The resting membrane potential of a real cell (about −70 mV) differs from the Nernst potential of K⁺, and Na⁺ keeps trying to flow in. Why are the concentration differences kept anyway? (Hint: the steady state of 18, ATP in section 4.10, the sodium–potassium pump)
Deriving the Nernst equation

The electrochemical potential of one ion is μ̃ = μ° + kT ln(c/c°) + zeψ. With the same reference inside and out, equilibrium μ̃in = μ̃out gives 0 = kT ln(cin/cout) + ze(ψin − ψout). Hence cin/cout = e−zeΔψ/kT, i.e. Δψ = (kT/ze) ln(cout/cin). Per mole, kT → RT and e → the Faraday constant FA, giving equation (4.16) of the textbook.

Away from equilibrium, if μ̃in − μ̃out > 0, moving ions out lowers the free energy, so there is a net outward movement (and inward in the opposite case). Check that the signs of "μ̃in − μ̃out" and "Net outward rate" in the table agree.

About the model

2D hard-disk gas (m = 1, k = 1). When an ion crosses the membrane, its speed perpendicular to the membrane changes by the difference in electrical energy zeΔψ (it bounces back if that is not enough). This is the same mechanism as the steps in 11, and kinetic + electrical energy is conserved exactly. The surrounding walls exchange heat with surroundings at temperature 1, so the temperature stays nearly constant.

This model leaves out the electrical forces between ions (Coulomb forces) and treats the membrane potential Δψ as a value given from outside (corresponding to an experiment that clamps the potential). In a real cell the membrane potential is made by a tiny difference in charge accumulated on the two sides of the membrane, negligibly small compared with the concentrations.
