Goal

In 11 we found the sedimentation equilibrium distribution c(z) ∝ e−mbgz/kT from the minimum of the free energy. What thermodynamics tells us is the destination (the equilibrium distribution). It does not tell us how fast the system gets there. The speed depends on the diffusion coefficient D and the mobility b (set by the viscosity), information separate from thermodynamics (textbook section 4.5).

However, D and b cannot be chosen freely. For the distribution where the flow stops to agree with the equilibrium distribution, we need D = bkT (the Einstein relation).

Try it

  1. Play. In both containers the distribution goes from uniform toward an exponential that is dense at the bottom. The destination (dashed) is the same, but the right one, with k times the viscosity, approaches k times more slowly.
  2. The free energy difference plot: both keep decreasing and stop at 0 (equilibrium). It never increases (the second law).
  3. The flow breakdown: at first the downward flow due to gravity wins. As the distribution becomes uneven, the upward diffusion flow grows, and in equilibrium they cancel exactly at every height. Particles keep moving, yet the net flow is 0.
  4. Start from "Gathered near the top". Even from a different initial state, the destination is the same.
  5. Change P. The larger P (heavier particles or a taller container), the more they collect at the bottom and the faster they approach equilibrium. P < 0 means floating particles.
  6. Move r away from 1 (break the Einstein relation). The distribution where the flow stops becomes e−Px/r and departs from the Boltzmann distribution. The free energy difference does not reach 0 either.

The flow equation and time scales

The upward particle flow is the sum of diffusion (Fick's law) and movement due to gravity, J = −D ∂c/∂z − b mbg c (eq. 4.6). Counting what enters and leaves a thin layer gives ∂c/∂t = −∂J/∂z (eq. 4.8). With x = z/H and τ = tD/H², ∂c/∂τ = ∂²c/∂x² + (P/r) ∂c/∂x, P = mbgH/kT, r = D/(bkT).

The time scales are tD ~ H²/D for diffusion to spread over the height H (τ = 1), and tg = H/(b mb g) for settling across H (τ = r/P). These are rough scales, not the exact time to approach equilibrium.

From Stokes drag b = 1/(6πηa), D = kT/(6πηa). Multiplying the viscosity η by k makes both D and b 1/k, so the destination is the same and only the time becomes k times longer.

About the model

This page is not a simulation of colliding particles (molecular dynamics) but a model of Brownian motion. Water molecules are not drawn; their effect is represented by random kicks (diffusion) and drag (mobility). Each step, a particle's height moves by Δx = −(P/r) Δτ + √(2Δτ) ξ (ξ is a random number with mean 0 and variance 1) and bounces back at the bottom and top. The horizontal motion is only for display.

The lines solve the same eq. (4.8) with the height split into 120 layers (the same method as the textbook's accompanying program sedimentation.py: a flux form that keeps equilibrium exact, and an implicit time step). Tests confirm agreement with that program's results to 6 digits at P = 5. Real lengths and times (3D, SI units) are converted with the particle of Example 4.
