Goal

Treating the whole Earth as one system, the first law reads "heat capacity × rate of temperature change = energy coming in − energy going out", CA dT/dt = Jin − Jout. The sign of this budget decides which way the temperature changes. What comes in is absorbed sunlight; what goes out is infrared radiation to space (proportional to the fourth power of temperature: the Stefan–Boltzmann law).

When the atmosphere absorbs the surface's infrared radiation (ε), less radiation escapes to space, and the surface satisfies a new budget at a higher temperature (the greenhouse effect). Where the budget balances is a steady state. It is not equilibrium: energy keeps flowing in from the Sun and out to space.

Try it

  1. Set ε = 0. The surface temperature approaches Te ≈ 255 K. What happens when you go back to ε = 0.8? And with ε = 1?
  2. Raise ε while running. The top-of-atmosphere budget becomes positive (more comes in), the surface warms, and the budget returns to 0. What sets how fast the temperature changes meanwhile?
  3. Change the mixed-layer depth (heat capacity). Does the final temperature change? The time to get there?
  4. Lower plot: doubling CO₂ (C/C₀ = 2) gives ΔJ ≈ 3.7 W m⁻². As you increase the feedback f, what happens to the final warming and the time constant? What happens if f exceeds B?
Question Downward radiation from the cold atmosphere reaches the warm surface. Can we say "heat flows from cold to warm, so the second law is violated"? Check the direction of the net radiative exchange between surface and atmosphere with the numbers in the diagram.
Question Even with the same radiation leaving to space (a steady state), the surface temperature depends on ε. Why? Check that the budget balances whether you draw the boundary around the surface, the atmosphere, or the whole Earth.
Question Even with a positive feedback (f > 0), the temperature does not rise without bound as long as f < B. If it warms a little too much, which way does the net budget push it back?
Summary of formulas

Absorbed solar radiation Jabs = (1−α)S₀/4 (the 1/4 is the ratio of the Earth's cross-section to its surface area). Effective radiating temperature σTe⁴ = Jabs.

One-layer model: from the atmosphere budget εσTs⁴ = 2εσTa⁴ and the top-of-atmosphere budget Jabs = (1−ε)σTs⁴ + εσTa⁴, Ts = Te(1 − ε/2)−1/4.

Time evolution: assuming the atmosphere reaches radiative equilibrium quickly (Ta⁴ = Ts⁴/2), the surface temperature is advanced with CA dTs/dt = Jabs − (1 − ε/2)σTs⁴. Linearizing around the steady state gives the restoring coefficient Bs = 4(1 − ε/2)σTs³ and time constant τ = CA/B.

The CO₂ forcing ΔJ = 5.35 ln(C/C₀) W m⁻² is an approximation obtained from radiative calculations including absorption spectra, not derived from the laws of thermodynamics. The feedback f (water vapor etc.) is also given separately from radiative calculations. J in this material is energy flow per unit area (written J to distinguish it from the free energy F).
