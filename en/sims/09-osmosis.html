Goal

A membrane that lets the solvent (water) through but not the solute is called a semipermeable membrane. If pure solvent and a solution are separated by a semipermeable membrane, solvent moves into the solution side (osmosis) and stops once the pressure on the solution side has become higher. The pressure difference at that point is the osmotic pressure Π. For a dilute solution, Π = (n/V)kT (the van 't Hoff equation), which has the same form as the equation of state of an ideal gas.

Try it

  1. Press "Make the partition semipermeable". How do the numbers of solvent particles (blue) on the left and right change? What about the left and right pressures?
  2. Wait a while, then compare Π (measured) with nkT/V. Press "Record".
  3. Change the number of solute particles n or the temperature T and repeat "Reset" → semipermeable → Record. Do the points on the graph lie on a straight line?
  4. Press "Push the piston at this pressure". If the pushing pressure is larger than the pressure on the solution side, only the solvent is squeezed out of the solution to the left (reverse osmosis). The solution becomes more concentrated and Π grows.
  5. What if you reduce the pushing pressure? Solvent flows into the right side, and the piston is pushed back.

Osmotic pressure in this model

Solvent particles pass straight through the membrane, so at equilibrium the solvent density (the solvent's "partial pressure") is the same on both sides. The pressure on the right is higher by the partial pressure of the solute, nkT/V. Therefore Π = PR − PL = nkT/V. Only the solute pushes on the membrane, so the "pressure of solute on the membrane" is also Π.

In a real solution the solvent is a liquid and solvent molecules exert forces on one another, but when the solute is dilute the same formula Π = cRT (c is the molar concentration) holds. It follows from the condition that the chemical potential of the solvent is equal on both sides of the membrane.

On this page the particles are made smaller to approach a dilute solution. Even so, the particles have a finite size, so in a more concentrated solution the measured Π comes out slightly larger than nkT/V. This formula has the same form in 2D and 3D (kT per particle).
