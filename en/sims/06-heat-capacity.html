Goal

The heat needed to raise the temperature of an object by 1 is called its heat capacity C = Q/ΔT. Even for the same gas, the value differs depending on whether you heat it at constant volume or at constant pressure. Think about the reason in terms of the work done via the piston.

Try it

  1. Measure at "Constant volume". What is C/N in the table? Measure several times with different reservoir temperatures and see which line the points in the graph fall on.
  2. Measure with different N. C is proportional to N (an extensive variable), but C/N does not change.
  3. Switch to "Constant pressure" and measure. The piston moves outward and the gas does work on the outside (W < 0). What is C/N now?
  4. Also try a "cooling" measurement with the reservoir temperature below 1 (Q < 0, ΔT < 0).
Question Heating at constant pressure needs more heat than at constant volume. Where did the extra heat go? Explain it with the first law, ΔU = Q + W.
Question Even if you separate the gas from the reservoir before it reaches the reservoir temperature, C still comes out right. Why?
Question If you repeat the same measurement several times, C/N scatters a little (especially at constant pressure with small N). What is fluctuating?
Theoretical values (2D monatomic ideal gas)

Since U = NkT, at constant volume Q = ΔU and CV = Nk (C/N = 1).

At constant pressure the gas does work PΔV = NkΔT on the outside, so Q = ΔU + PΔV and CP = 2Nk (C/N = 2). CP − CV = Nk (Mayer's relation), γ = CP/CV = 2.

For a 3D monatomic ideal gas, CV = (3/2)Nk and CP = (5/2)Nk. The difference is the number of degrees of freedom per particle (2 in 2D, 3 in 3D).

In this simulation the temperature is defined by T = U/N, so C/N at constant volume comes out exactly 1. At constant pressure it scatters by a few percent because of piston fluctuations (energy exchange between the gas and the weight). Cooling at constant pressure makes the volume smaller, and the effect of particle size (deviation from an ideal gas) makes C/N come out slightly below 2.
