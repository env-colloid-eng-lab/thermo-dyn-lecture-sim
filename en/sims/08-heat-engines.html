Goal

A device that repeatedly takes heat Qh from a hot reservoir, turns part of it into work, and discards the rest to a cold reservoir is a heat engine. Run in reverse, it becomes a heat pump (refrigerator), which uses work to pump heat from the cold side to the hot side.

Try it

  1. Run a few cycles with "Carnot" and "Heat engine". The area enclosed by the cycle on the P–V diagram is the work done on the surroundings per cycle. Compare the measured efficiency η with the theory 1 − Tc/Th.
  2. Change the piston speed to "Slow" or "Fast". What happens to the efficiency when it is fast? (Recall the irreversible processes in 03.)
  3. What happens to the efficiency when you lower Tc or raise Th?
  4. Switch to "Heat pump". Work is done on the gas from outside; the gas takes heat from the cold side and gives off more heat to the hot side. The coefficient of performance (COP) is greater than 1.
  5. "Stirling": the isothermal processes are connected by constant-volume processes. Without a regenerator (a heat-storage material that holds the heat exchanged at constant volume), the efficiency drops. In the heat-pump direction, without a regenerator the device may not even be able to pump heat out of the cold side (look at Q in the table).
  6. Look at the T–S diagram. The Carnot cycle is a rectangle (isothermal = horizontal, adiabatic = vertical). The heat received on the hot side is ThΔS, the heat discarded to the cold side is TcΔS, and the enclosed area is the work done on the surroundings. Hence η = 1 − Tc/Th.
  7. Look at the total of "ΔS of reservoir" in the table. The gas returns to its initial state after one cycle, so the total entropy change is just that of the reservoirs. It is ≈ 0 for a slow Carnot cycle and positive (irreversible) when the piston is fast or for Stirling without a regenerator.
Question The total entropy change of the reservoirs in one cycle, −Qh/Th − Qc/Tc, is never negative (the second law, Clausius inequality). From this and the first law −W = Qh + Qc, derive that the efficiency cannot exceed η ≤ 1 − Tc/Th.
Question Under every setting, the efficiency never exceeds the "Carnot limit". Explain why a heat engine that exceeded it would contradict the second law (the Clausius principle and the Thomson principle).
Question In a heat pump, the heat delivered to the hot side is larger than the work done. Why doesn't this contradict energy conservation? Compare with an electric heater (COP = 1).
Question The efficiency varies quite a lot from cycle to cycle. Why does the total over several cycles get closer to the theoretical value?
The T–S diagram and the entropy budget

The entropy of a 2D ideal gas is S = Nk ln V + Nk ln T + const. In a quasi-static process the heat received by the gas is δQ = T dS, so the area under a curve on the T–S diagram is heat, and the area enclosed by one cycle is the net heat (= work done on the surroundings, −W). The T in the plot is computed from the particles' kinetic energy and slightly smoothed (with few particles it still fluctuates).

The entropy change of a reservoir (constant temperature) is the heat it received, −Q, divided by its temperature: −Q/Treservoir. The gas returns to its initial state after one cycle (ΔSgas = 0), so the total entropy change is the sum over the reservoirs, −Qh/Th − Qc/Tc ≥ 0. Equality holds when the cycle is reversible (a slowly run Carnot cycle). It becomes positive when heat flows across a finite temperature difference between the gas and a reservoir. With few particles the value for a single cycle fluctuates a lot (and the gas does not return exactly to its initial state), so it can even be negative, but averaged over many cycles it is 0 or more.

Theoretical values (2D ideal gas, γ = 2)

In an isothermal process (temperature T, volume V₁ → V₂), the heat received by the gas is Q = NkT ln(V₂/V₁). In an adiabatic process, TV = const.

Carnot: Qh = NkTh ln r, −Qc = NkTc ln r (r is the volume ratio of the isothermal expansion; r = 2 on this page). Efficiency η = 1 − Tc/Th; coefficient of performance of the heat pump COP = Th/(Th − Tc).

Stirling: the constant-volume processes exchange heat Nk(Th − Tc). Without a regenerator this heat is also exchanged with the reservoirs, so η = (Th − Tc) ln r / (Th ln r + Th − Tc). With an ideal regenerator, the constant-volume heat just goes back and forth within the cycle, and the efficiency equals the Carnot efficiency.

For the reverse Stirling cycle (heat-pump direction) without a regenerator, the net heat received from the cold side is Nk(Tc ln r − (Th − Tc)). If Tc ln r < Th − Tc, this is negative: the work done from outside is simply dumped as heat into both reservoirs, and the device does not work as a heat pump (the COP shown is then less than 1).

The pressure on the P–V diagram is drawn from the equation of state NkT/V. When the piston moves fast, the gas near the moving piston is out of equilibrium, and the pressure the piston actually feels differs from NkT/V. For this reason, the shaded area and the net work −W in the table (counted collision by collision) agree well only when the piston moves slowly.

In the simulation the piston speed is finite and there is a temperature difference between the gas and the reservoirs, so the measured values are smaller than the theoretical ones. Because the number of particles is small, the value for each single cycle fluctuates a lot.
