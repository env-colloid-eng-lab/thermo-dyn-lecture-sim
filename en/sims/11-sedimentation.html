Goal

Take two regions of equal size, where only the particles in the upper region have an energy higher by ΔE (the top and bottom of a container under gravity). How do the particles split between them?

  • Counting arrangements alone (configurational entropy), half and half is the most likely.
  • Considering energy alone, all particles at the bottom is the lowest.

In reality the system settles in between. If it is in contact with a reservoir (temperature T), what decides it is the minimum of the free energy F = U − TS, and the fraction x in the upper region satisfies x/(1−x) = e−ΔE/kT. This restates "maximum entropy of system + reservoir" (the second law) using quantities of the system alone. For an isolated system, it stops where the total entropy is maximal. Making the layers finer gives sedimentation equilibrium under gravity, c(z) ∝ e−z/ℓ (ℓ = kT/mg: the gravitational length).

Try it

  1. Play with "Two regions" and "In contact with a reservoir". x decreases from 1/2 and fluctuates around the theoretical value. The black dot in the F(x) plot heads for the bottom of the valley.
  2. What happens with ΔE = 0? With a larger ΔE? With a higher reservoir temperature T? (x depends only on ΔE/kT.) With ΔE < 0 the top has the lower energy (particles that float in water).
  3. Look at the "Entropy budget". As particles gather at the bottom, the configurational entropy of the system decreases. Even so, heat flows out into the reservoir, the reservoir's entropy increases, and the total increases.
  4. Switch to "Adiabatic (isolated system)". As particles move down, potential energy turns into kinetic energy and the gas warms up ("Color = speed"). This time it stops where the total entropy S(x) is maximal.
  5. Switch to "Many layers (approx. gravity)" and increase the number of layers n. The concentration in each layer approaches the smooth exponential e−z/ℓ. What happens when you change mgH or T?
  6. Changing ΔE during a run changes W in the ledger. Changing the step is work done from outside, lifting (or lowering) the particles.

Free energy of two regions

The number of arrangements with Nx of the N particles in the upper region is W = N!/((Nx)!(N−Nx)!). Stirling's approximation gives S_conf = −Nk[x ln x + (1−x) ln(1−x)]. At fixed temperature T the part from the speed distribution does not depend on x, so

F/N = xΔE + kT[x ln x + (1−x) ln(1−x)] + const

Setting the derivative with respect to x to 0 gives ΔE + kT ln(x/(1−x)) = 0, i.e. x = 1/(1 + eΔE/kT). The second derivative is positive, so this is a minimum. The plot shows F per particle, as the difference from its value at x = 1/2.

In an isolated system the total energy E is fixed. In 2D the kinetic energy is NkT, so raising the fraction x in the upper region lowers the temperature to T = E/N − xΔE. The maximum of the total entropy S/N = −k[x ln x + (1−x) ln(1−x)] + k ln T satisfies the same relation x/(1−x) = e−ΔE/kT at the final temperature.

Many layers and sedimentation equilibrium (gravitational length)

Divide the container into n layers and give a particle in the k-th layer from the bottom the energy k·mgH/n (a staircase approximation of the gravitational potential energy mgz). The same condition as for two regions holds between any two neighboring layers, so the fraction is proportional to e−k·mgH/(nkT). As n grows this becomes the continuous distribution c(z) = c(0) e−z/ℓ, ℓ = kT/mg. The dotted line in the plot is for continuous gravity.

What becomes constant in equilibrium is not the concentration but the chemical potential, which combines the concentration term and the potential energy term: kT ln c(z) + mgz. The same formula explains why the pressure (density) of an isothermal atmosphere decreases exponentially with height.

How the entropy budget is counted

The configurational entropy of the system is the number of ways to split the particles among the layers, ln(N!/ΠN_k!) (all layers have the same size). The part from the speeds is Nk ln T for a 2D ideal gas. The entropy change of the reservoir is the heat it received divided by its temperature (the heat Q received by the gas with the sign reversed). The temperature shown is slightly smoothed. Because the number of particles is small, every quantity fluctuates.

About the model

When a particle reaches the boundary of a step, it moves into the upper layer if its vertical kinetic energy exceeds the step ΔE, losing that much vertical speed; otherwise it bounces back (going down, it speeds up). The potential energy depends only on the layer number, so the sum of kinetic and potential energy is conserved exactly. The boundary is not a wall but a step in energy.

The particles are disks of finite size, so in the crowded lower layer they are a little more "cramped" than an ideal gas, and the fraction in the upper region comes out slightly (about 1%) larger than the theoretical value.
