Goal

In 02 and 11, a gas left alone settled into equilibrium and stopped changing there. In equilibrium the total entropy is maximal and neither heat nor matter flows on balance.

What happens if we put a hot reservoir below and a cold reservoir above? After a while the temperature distribution of the gas stops changing. But this is not equilibrium: heat keeps flowing from hot to cold and the total entropy keeps increasing. This is called a steady state (non-equilibrium steady state). The Earth's climate (15, 17) and living cells are also "steady states sustained by a flow".

Try it

  1. At first both reservoirs are at T = 1 (the same temperature). Q̇h and −Q̇c only fluctuate around 0 and the temperature is the same at every height. This is equilibrium.
  2. Press "Temperature difference" (Th = 2, Tc = 0.5). After a while the temperature settles into a profile that is higher near the bottom and dU/dt ≈ 0. Still Q̇h ≈ −Q̇c > 0: as much heat goes out as comes in.
  3. In the lower plot, check that the total entropy of the reservoirs keeps increasing at a constant rate. The state of the gas does not change, yet entropy keeps being produced overall.
  4. The "crowding" plot: particles are more crowded near the cold top. To keep the pressure (nearly the same at all heights), more particles are needed where the temperature is lower.
  5. Press "Remove". The heat flow stops and the temperature profile is smoothed out until it is uniform (back to equilibrium). A steady state cannot be kept without a continuous flow of heat from outside.
  6. Halve the temperature difference. How do Q̇ and σ change? (σ is roughly proportional to the square of the temperature difference.)

Energy and entropy ledger

First law: dU/dt = Q̇h + Q̇c (the piston is held still, so W = 0). In a steady state dU/dt = 0, so Q̇h = −Q̇c ≡ Q̇.

The entropy change of a reservoir is the heat it received divided by its temperature: dSres/dt = −Q̇h/Th − Q̇c/Tc. In a steady state the gas's entropy does not change, so the total rate of entropy production is σ = Q̇ (1/Tc − 1/Th) ≥ 0. It is positive only when heat flows from hot to cold (Q̇ > 0, Th > Tc). This is the direction of flow set by the second law.

For a small temperature difference the heat rate is proportional to the temperature difference (Fourier's law Q̇ ∝ Th − Tc). Then σ is proportional to the square of the temperature difference.

Equilibrium vs. steady state (textbook section 8.7)

Sedimentation equilibrium (11) is an equilibrium that lasts without any supply from outside: the total entropy is maximal and there is no net flow. The radiative-convective equilibrium of the climate (17) has "equilibrium" in its name, but it is a steady state sustained by energy coming in from the Sun and going out to space, and it keeps producing entropy. The gas on this page is the simplest example of the latter.

About the model

2D hard-disk gas (m = 1, k = 1, U = NkT). The particles are larger than on the other pages (radius 1) so that heat is relayed by collisions between particles. When a particle hits a reservoir wall it bounces back with a speed drawn anew from that reservoir's temperature distribution (heat exchange seen microscopically). The ledger adds up the energy change of every single collision, so ΔU = Q + W holds exactly.

The temperature of the layers right next to the walls differs a little from the reservoir temperature (a temperature jump at the wall), because particles hit the wall a finite number of times and the heat exchange with the wall is not complete. The more particles (the denser the gas), the smaller the jump. Because the particles have a size, the equation of state deviates a little from the ideal gas (the product of crowding and temperature changes slightly with height).
