Goal

Quasi-static process: a process built up from tiny changes while the fluid pressure and the force moving the piston always stay balanced. It is reversible (the reverse operation brings it back). A fast operation, on the other hand, breaks equilibrium along the way and becomes irreversible.

Try it

  1. With "Adiabatic" and "Quasi-static", do one round trip (you may raise the playback speed). When the piston comes back, has T returned to its original value? What is W for the round trip?
  2. Do one round trip with "Adiabatic" and "Very fast". What now? How do orange and blue separate on the P–V diagram?
  3. Compress with "Isothermal" and "Quasi-static". W > 0, yet ΔU ≈ 0. Where did the energy go? (→ 04)
  4. Look at the "Potential temperature θ" readout. In a quasi-static adiabatic round trip θ stays constant; in a very fast round trip θ rises. Isothermal compression lowers θ. What does θ represent? (See "Potential temperature and entropy" below.)
  5. Free expansion: set it up and remove the partition. What are W, Q, ΔU, and T? Can you draw the intermediate states on the P–V diagram?
Question Even when surrounded by adiabatic walls, a fast round trip makes the gas's U increase (an irreversible adiabatic process). Conversely, is there an operation that keeps everything adiabatic and only lowers U to bring the gas back?
Question In a fast compression, particles "pile up" in front of the piston, and the pressure on the piston becomes larger than NkT/V. What does that do to the work? What about a fast expansion?
Comparison with theory (2D ideal gas)

For a 2D monatomic ideal gas, U = NkT, CV = Nk, γ = 2.

  • Adiabatic quasi-static: PV² = const., TV = const. (halving the volume doubles T)
  • Isothermal quasi-static: W = NkT ln(V₀/V₁), ΔU = 0, Q = −W

The particles in this simulation have a finite size (hard disks), so under strong compression the pressure is a few percent higher than for an ideal gas.

Potential temperature and entropy (toward atmospheric temperature)

The temperature a gas at pressure P and temperature T would have after a quasi-static adiabatic change to a reference pressure P₀ is the potential temperature θ. For a 2D ideal gas cP = 2k (per particle), so θ = T(P₀/P)k/cP = T(P₀/P)1/2. For 3D dry air the exponent is R/cp ≈ 0.286.

The entropy can be written S = NcP ln θ + const (for the gas on this page ΔS = 2Nk ln(θ/θ₀)). In a quasi-static adiabatic change both S and θ stay constant. In a fast round trip (an irreversible adiabatic process) S increases, so θ rises. In isothermal compression heat leaves, so S decreases and θ falls.

In the atmosphere a rising air parcel expands and cools, but keeps its θ. Comparing potential temperatures instead of temperatures shows which air is "really warmer" (has larger entropy), and tells whether convection occurs.
