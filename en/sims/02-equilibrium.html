Goal

If two fluids at different temperatures are put in contact through a diathermal wall and left alone, they eventually settle into a state with no macroscopic change (an equilibrium state). We look at the lecture's "coffee and room" example microscopically.

Energy conservation alone does not decide whether energy moves 1→2 or 2→1. The direction is decided by the second law (the entropy principle): when an internal constraint of an isolated composite system (here, the adiabatic wall) is removed, the change proceeds in the direction in which the total entropy S₁ + S₂ increases, and the state where it is maximal is the equilibrium state.

Try it

  1. Wait with the adiabatic wall in place. Do T₁ and T₂ change? Does the black dot in the S(U₁) plot move? (The adiabatic wall is a "constraint" that forbids exchanging energy.)
  2. Switch to "Diathermal wall". Which way does the energy move? How does the particle speed change at the wall? ("Color = speed" makes this easy to see.)
  3. Check that the black dot climbs the S(U₁) curve. Where does it stop? What are 1/T₁ and 1/T₂ there?
  4. Wait long enough. What happens to T₁ and T₂? Do U₁ and U₂ become equal? (Try it with N₁ ≠ N₂. The position of the top of the curve changes too.)
  5. Even after reaching equilibrium, the particles keep moving. What are the small fluctuations in the graphs?
  6. Finally, "Remove" the partition. The two fluids mix. Can you wait until they return to the original state?

The entropy curve (2D ideal gas)

For a 2D ideal gas with fixed volume and particle number, S = Nk ln U + (a constant set by V and N). Differentiating with respect to U gives ∂S/∂U = Nk/U = 1/T (U = NkT). This "slope of the entropy is 1/T" is used as the definition of thermodynamic temperature.

Differentiating the total entropy with respect to U₁ gives dS/dU₁ = 1/T₁ − 1/T₂. If T₁ > T₂ it is negative, so S increases in the direction where U₁ decreases (energy moves from 1 to 2). The top, dS/dU₁ = 0, is T₁ = T₂. The curve is concave (bulges upward), so there is only one top, and it is the stable equilibrium.

The ΔS in the plot is the change from the initial state, ΔS = N₁ ln(U₁/U₁⁰) + N₂ ln(U₂/U₂⁰) (k = 1). The curve is drawn with the current total gas energy U₁ + U₂ (this total changes slightly by what the partition wall temporarily stores). Because the number of particles is finite, the dot fluctuates a little around equilibrium.

About the model

The diathermal wall is modeled as "a wall with a small heat capacity". When a particle hits the wall, it bounces back with a velocity drawn from the Maxwell distribution at the wall temperature Tw, and the wall's energy changes by the same amount. Particles on both sides exchange energy with the same wall, so heat flows between the left and right fluids through the wall (the total energy of gas + wall is conserved exactly).
